# Executive Summary

When the Coral Sea Marine Park was established knowledge about the number and location of the reefs was limited. The size and extent of the large coral atoll platforms was known, along with large shallow reefs, but no detail was known about the many small lagoonal patch reefs, or the deeper reefs. The Coral Sea Marine Park management plan described the parks as having ‘approximately 34 reefs, and 56 cays and islets’ (Director of National Parks, 2018).

Having access to detailed maps of the size, depth and distribution of reefs in the Coral Sea is vital foundational information for marine park planning. It helps establish how representative management plans and zoning are of key habitats. It helps with planning marine park monitoring and scientific research and with communicating to broader communities the scale of these reef systems.

The goal of this project was to develop, for the Coral Sea, a similar reef boundary dataset as the Great Barrier Reef Features dataset used by the Great Barrier Reefs Marine Park Authority for the last 25 years.

In this project we combined the best available satellite imagery to create image composites optimised for seeing as deep into the water column as possible (Lawrey & Hammerton, 2022). We selected and combined a collection of the clearest Sentinel 2 and Landsat 8-9 imagery to create low noise image composites. This allowed the faint signal of deep reefs to be visible. In many parts of the Coral Sea reef features as deep as 50 – 60 m were visible as dark patches compared to the surrounding sand. This is twice as deep as the PlanetLabs image mosaics of the Coral Sea developed for the Allen Coral Atlas (Lyons et al., 2020). This allowed significant portions of the atoll lagoonal floor to be directly viewed in the satellite imagery, allowing comprehensive mapping of the thousands of both shallow and deep (\> 30 m) small lagoonal patch reefs.

Most of the reefs of the central Coral Sea formed over last tens of millions of years as the Queensland Plateau slowly subsided from above sea level to its present depth of 500 – 1000 m (McKenzie et al., 1993). As the land subsided coral reefs formed on rocky foundations, growing upwards, matching the pace of the subsidence. During the last million years the sea level has fallen and risen with glacial cycles (Spratt & Lisiecki, 2016), with the most recent ice age resulting in sea levels dropping 130 m (Lambeck et al., 2014). During low sea level periods the tops of the atoll platforms become hills, and rainwater dissolved the carbonate karst structures (Droxler & Jorry, 2021; Mylroie & Carew, 2003). When the sea level rose again coral repopulated high points. Low areas, the lagoonal floor, were quickly covered in carbonate sediment, preventing coral growth. The peaks grew, matching the rising sea level, forming the thousands of lagoonal patch reefs we find today.

While all the reefs of the Coral Sea are built on top of large, tall atoll platforms we chose to map the reefs shallower than 60 m separately from the atoll platforms themselves. Mapped coral reef boundaries were based on hard coral substrate areas where hard coral might be found, separated from other reefs by soft sediment. Patches closer than 200 m apart where clustered together into a larger mapped boundary. This was to prevent the large number of sparse reef patches on the sandy backs of rim reefs from being mapped as separate reefs.

While all the lagoonal and rim reefs on an atoll platform share a common solid core framework with the atoll platform itself, we consider them as separate from an ecological perspective due to the surrounding sediment on the lagoonal floor creating a habitat transition.
