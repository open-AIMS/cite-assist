## 1.1 Environmental-management problem

Coral and rocky reefs are biodiversity hotspots in the marine environment (Roberts et al., 2002). The structural complexity provided by consolidated reef substrates supports substantially higher species richness and biomass than surrounding soft sediments (Graham & Nash, 2013). These habitats are home to diverse assemblages of corals, fish, invertebrates and macroalgae, and their presence is a strong indicator of high conservation value.

Northern and western Australia's marine estate encompasses over ten thousand kilometres of coastline, from the Houtman Abrolhos off Western Australia to western Cape York in Queensland. This vast seascape contains thousands of coral and rocky reefs, yet prior to this project many were absent from publicly available spatial datasets. This absence creates a practical problem for environmental management: reefs that do not appear in any reference dataset risk being overlooked during the planning and assessment of coastal and offshore developments.

Environmental impact assessments rely on spatial data to identify sensitive habitats that may be affected by proposed activities (Lyons et al., 2020). When reef mapping is incomplete, proponents may fail to identify affected habitats during initial site selection, or discover them only late in the assessment process. Late discovery typically leads to project delays, costly redesigns and potentially inadequate environmental protections. For regulators, incomplete mapping reduces the ability to assess whether a proponent has adequately identified all potentially affected habitats.

The datasets produced by this project are intended to serve as a regional-scale screening tool. They flag locations where reef habitats may occur and where more detailed investigation may be required. They do not replace site-specific impact assessment or field surveys. Their value lies in reducing the risk that reefs are missed entirely during early-stage planning, allowing appropriate levels of investigation to be triggered before commitments are made.

Beyond development assessment, comprehensive reef mapping supports marine park management, scientific research planning and national habitat accounting. Marine park managers require knowledge of habitat distribution to design effective zoning plans and assess cumulative impacts (Carter et al., 2021; Bozec et al., 2022). Researchers planning field surveys need to know where different reef types occur to design representative sampling strategies (Mastrantonis et al., 2024). Prior to this mapping, researchers often had to conduct preliminary surveys simply to locate suitable study sites in many parts of northern Australia.
