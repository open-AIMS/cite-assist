## Previous mapping of the Coral Sea

The mapping of the Coral Sea has been limited and fragmented compared to the extensively documented Great Barrier Reef. This disparity stems from historical priorities that focused surveying efforts on economically important navigation routes rather than comprehensive habitat documentation. Understanding this mapping history reveals the significant knowledge gaps that existed prior to the current project.

The earliest systematic mapping of the Coral Sea began in the mid-19th century through British Admiralty hydrographic surveys. Between 1815 and 1860, naval expeditions concentrated primarily on charting safe shipping routes through the Great Barrier Reef to Torres Strait, with only limited attention to the broader Coral Sea (Morgan, 2021).

Historic British and American Admiralty charts from 1860 through 1960 reveal the incremental and incomplete nature of Coral Sea documentation. The earliest charts (1860-1861) marked major reef systems such as Tregrosse Reefs and Coringa Islets as 'not surveyed' (Great Britain Hydrographic Dept, 1861), while others suffered from significant positioning errors due to limitations in longitude calculations (Great Britain Hydrographic Department, 1860). We see that maps from 1956 (Great Britain. Hydrographic Department, 1956) are virtually unchanged from those developed in 1860, large atoll platforms remained poorly represented or unmapped in particular Magdelaine Cays and Coringa Islets were mapped only as isolated cays, with no recognition of their connection to a common platform.

The first comprehensive geomorphic mapping of the Coral Sea emerged was compiled from a high-resolution bathymetric model (the gbr100 grid) at 100-meter resolution covering approximately 3 million km² (Beaman, 2010). This project revealed that prior to 2010, approximately one-third of deep-water areas (\>200 m) lacked adequate spatial data, and most shallow inter-reefal areas had never been mapped with modern echosounder techniques (Beaman, 2010). Building on this bathymetric foundation, the geomorphic boundaries were manually digitised for the Great Barrier Reef and the Coral Sea, providing the first systematic mapping of atoll platform boundaries and dry reef extents (Beaman, 2012). However, this work explicitly acknowledged that small features were "greatly underestimated" due to the limited resolution of the underlying bathymetry data (Beaman, 2012).

In 2020, the Schmidt Ocean Institute's RV *Falkor* expedition conducted extensive multibeam sonar surveys around the Queensland Plateau, mapping over 35,000 km² with resolutions of 16–64 meters (Beaman et al., 2020). This survey targeted steep reef flanks and deep plateau features, revealing submarine canyons, drowned reef pinnacles, and debris fields around platform perimeters. The expedition focused on deep reef morphology from the plateau base to reef crests rather than shallow lagoon interiors (Schmidt Ocean Institute, 2020). Consequently, while the external architecture of the major atoll platforms became well-characterized, the thousands of small lagoonal reefs within these platforms remained unmapped at comparable resolution.

Prior to the current project, the highest resolution representation of the reefs of the Coral Sea was provided by the Australian Hydrographic Office marine chart products. These were however designed for navigation safety rather than habitat management. This project addresses this fundamental gap through systematic satellite-based mapping of reef features across all reef platforms in the Coral Sea Marine Park.
